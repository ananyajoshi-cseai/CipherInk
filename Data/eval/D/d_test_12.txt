I endeavour to give satisfaction.” About a week after this, Bingo blew in with the news that his uncle’s gout had ceased to trouble him, and that on the morrow he would be back at the old stand working away with knife and fork as before. “And, by the way,” said Bingo, “he wants you to lunch with him to-morrow.” “Me? Why me? He doesn’t know I exist.” “Oh, yes, he does. I’ve told him about you.” “What have you told him?” “Oh, various things. Anyhow, he wants to meet you. And take my tip, laddie--you go! I should think lunch to-morrow would be something special.” I don’t know why it was, but even then it struck me that there was something dashed odd--almost sinister, if you know what I mean--about young Bingo’s manner. The old egg had the air of one who has something up his sleeve. “There is more in this than meets the eye,” I said. “Why should your uncle ask a fellow to lunch whom he’s never seen?” “My dear old fathead, haven’t I just said that I’ve been telling him all about you--that you’re my best pal--at school together, and all that sort of thing?” “But even then--and another thing. Why are you so dashed keen on my going?” Bingo hesitated for a moment. “Well, I told you I’d got an idea. This is it. I want you to spring the news on him. I haven’t the nerve myself.” “What!
